The problems with the zero-copula construction

The zero-copula construction may sound simple, but it introduces three chief problems that leave Sindarin writers scrambling and divide opinion:

  1. There's no distinction between tenses
  2. There's no distinction between predication and attribution
  3. There's no distinction between predication of nouns and genitive constructions
Past and future tense copula

Paul Strack, creator of Eldamo, has attempted to address this issue, which he explains on his page about the copula.

The issue can be illustrated with the following three sentences: 

  Elrond is there
  Elrond was there
  Elrond will be there

Translating these into Sindarin using a zero copula yields the exact same translation to all three sentences: 

  Elrond ennas

How can that be disambiguated? Paul analysed the na- verb and figured that Tolkien actually left clues as to what that would look like in the past tense and future tense. With some educated guesses, he suggested the use of a past tense copula **nî** and a future tense copula **tho**, which would yield the following translations: 

  Elrond ennas     = "Elrond is there"
  Elrond nî ennas  = "Elrond was there"
  Elrond tho ennas = "Elrond will be there"

Some say that **tho** doesn't fit with the later etymology of the future suffix, so the alternative would be to use **natho** instead: 

  Elrond **natho** ennas = "Elrond **will be** there"
Predication and attribution

Predication is the grammatical relationship in which a predicate attributes a property, identity, state, or relation to a subject. In English, nominal and adjectival predicates typically require a copula. Examples: 

  the elf is tall
  Elrond is a warrior

An attribution refers to a word or word group, such as an adjective, that is used to modify a noun within a noun phrase, rather than simply placing a word next to another word. 

  the tall elf

A predicative sentence in Sindarin requires the use of a zero copula. The issue is that the translation for both constructions may be identical: 

  en·edhel §s{dond} = "the elf is tall"
  en·edhel §s{dond} = "the tall elf"

The solution that some Sindarin writers came up with is to not mutate an adjective if it's being used in a predicative sentence: 

  en·edhel **tond** = "the elf is tall" → unmutated, predicative
  en·edhel **§s{dond}** = "the tall elf"    → mutated, attributive

However, some people think this is not a good solution because this distinction has not been securely established in Tolkien's Sindarin writings, and mutation doesn't affect words starting in vowels.

An alternative solution is to reverse the order of the predicate: 

  **tond** en·edhel = "the elf is tall" = "tall [is] the elf" (lit.)
  en·edhel **§s{dond}** = "the tall elf"    = "the elf tall" (lit.)

Strack cites PE22/124 for predicate inversion in Common Eldarin, alongside an example from Noldorin involving the height of a door. This supports the plausibility of inverted predicates in Sindarin. However, it does not conclusively establish that the construction was productive in Tolkien's mature Sindarin, so it's a preferred Neo-Sindarin convention supported by comparative evidence, but not canonical Tolkien material.

Copula vs genitive

A genitive is a construction for expressing relations between two nouns. See the section below for more information.

In Sindarin, as explained below, a genitive is formed by positioning two nouns of a chain in sequence, next to each other, like so: 

  aran Moria = "[the] king [of] Moria"

The problem is that nouns can be used in predicative sentences, as much as adjectives do. The difference is that under the usual Neo-Sindarin convention, neither nominal nor adjectival predicates undergoes lenition, so the syntax for a zero copula with two nouns is identical to a genitive construction: 

  rhovan callon = "[a] beast [is] [a] hero" → copula
  rhovan callon = "[a] beast [of] [a] hero" → genitive

Fiona Jallings, author of *A Fan's Guide to Neo-Sindarin*, tried to tackle this problem by suggesting we use §s{soft mutation} for predicative sentences using the copula, using the opposite mechanic used with adjectives. This way it would be easy to disambiguate: 

  rhovan §s{gallon} = "[a] beast [is] [a] hero"
  rhovan callon = "[a] beast [of] [a] hero"

The problem with this approach, again, is that it's not attested anywhere. It's an invention. Another problem is that this doesn't work with nouns that start with vowels, as vowels don't mutate.

One possible solution is to use the definite article on the first noun to *force* a copula: 

  **e**·§s{throvan} callon = "**the** beast [is] [a] hero"

That occurs because the genitive only allows the article on the last noun.

To express "a beast is a hero", keeping it indefinite and disambiguated, some suggest using a word for "one", or "some". These words act like adjectives, however. Example: 

  rhovan **nodui** callon = "beast **some** [is] [a] hero"
  rhovan **er** callon    = "beast **one** [is] [a] hero"

There is no straight-forward solution to this problem.

Suggestions

To help disambiguate all the cases presented above, I suppose there are 3 best practices one should keep in mind:

a) use tense copulas

Paul Strack's solution for disambiguating the past, present and future tense copulas seems reasonable, and I think it should be used.

b) reverse adjectival predicates

Always reverse the adjectives in a predicative sentence to prevent making it attributive.

c) use the definite article to disambiguate from genitives

In a genitive chain, the article can only be used in the last noun of the chain, and it changes the definiteness of the whole chain. If one uses the article in the first noun of the chain, it has to be interpreted as a predicative sentence: 

  **e**·§s{throvan} callon = "the beast [is] [a] hero"   → copula
  rhovan **e**·§s{gallon}  = "[the] beast [of] the hero" → genitive

The frowned upon path: Reconstruction of the "to be" verb

There are some very strong arguments for keeping the zero copula and it's very hard to find a Tolkien-oriented linguist who would be willing to play with a full-fledged reconstruction of a "to be" paradigm, so the reader will be hard-pressed to find any examples of it. However, it would solve a great many problems and I think it's worth giving it a try.

As it happens, there was at least one reconstruction that is worth looking at, by David Salo, but it's a bit outdated.

David Salo's "to be" verb paradigm

David Salo developed a whole system for Neo-Sindarin in the 1990s, published in 2004 in his book A Gateway to Sindarin (the "to be" reconstruction is on p. 121). Some of his interpretations, especially where he "filled gaps" in vocabulary or grammar, are now seen as inventions rather than canonical Tolkien material. Since then, at least eight subsequent issues have been published of Parma Eldalamberon have published a substantial amount of Tolkien's notes, revealing previously unknown details about Sindarin and rendering parts of Salo's work outdated. Many scholars and enthusiasts have since moved toward more conservative, strictly text-based reconstructions.

That said, David Salo is a competent linguist, and his work on the phonetic development of Sindarin and on its verb system was very valuable. One of the gaps he filled was the "to be" verb **na-**, which he used moderately in his translations for the Peter Jackson films. His reconstruction is extremely hypothetical, but it looks like this: 

  na- ("to be")
  
  Infinitive ..............: naw     - be (OS nōbe)
  Gerund ..................: nad     - being
  Imperative ..............: naw, no - be
  Present participle ......: naul    - being
  Past passive participle .: naun    - been
  Perfect active participle: nóniel  - having been

  **Present tense**

  1st person - naun  - I am
  3rd person - naw   - he/she/it is

  1st person - nam   - we are
  3rd person - nar   - they are

  **Past tense:**

  1st person - nónen - I was
  3rd person - naun  - he/she/it was (non)

  1st person - nónem - we were
  3rd person - nóner - they were

  **Future tense:**

  1st person - nathon - I will be
  3rd person - natha  - he/she/it will be

  1st person - natham - we will be
  3rd person - nathar - they will be

Salo's paradigm **is not** widely used, and many Neo-Sindarin users strongly oppose it. It is probably not compatible with Paul Strack's copulas either, and since it has very little support in Tolkien's writings, it's considered an invention. Still, it undeniably fixes all problems with predication and attribution: 

  Elrond ennas       = "Elrond is there"
  Elrond **naun** ennas  = "Elrond **was** there"
  Elrond **natha** ennas = "Elrond **will be** there"

  en·edhel **naw** §s{dond}  = "the elf **is** tall"
  en·edhel §s{dond}      = "the tall elf"

  rhovan **naw** §s{gallon} = "[a] beast **is** [a] hero"   → Salo's 'to be'
  rhovan callon     = "[a] beast [of] [a] hero" → genitive

A modern reconstruction of the "to be" verb

This is extremely speculative and not at all authoritative. If you want a conservative and source-based paradigm, walk away now! This is akin to my personal notes.

David Salo treated *na-* as a derived **A-stem verb**. Other Neo-Sindarin analysts treat it instead as a **monosyllabic basic verb**, derived directly from the primitive root **√NA** ("be, exist"). Under that model, each tense below is first derived from the regular sound laws, then checked against the few forms Tolkien actually wrote.

Present tense

The idea is to derive the present tense from **na-** as a basic verb, then use those forms to fill the gaps between the forms Tolkien actually wrote, even when they come from very early paradigms such as Gnomish, one of the ancestral forms of Sindarin.

  • **Derivation:**
    • **3rd person singular:** monosyllabic basic verbs take no suffix; instead, the stem vowel is lengthened (*sundóma* extension): *na-* → **nâ**.
    • **Other persons:** a connective (aorist) vowel *-i-* is inserted before the pronominal suffix, and it triggers i-affection (*prestanneth*) on the stem vowel, as in *car-* → *cerin* "I do": *na-* + *-i-* → **nai-** / **nei-**.
  • **Attested forms:**
    • **nâ** / **na** "is" (*PE17:59*, *VT49:28-30*). For existential statements ("there is"), Tolkien introduced **da-** / **đa** instead (*PE22:165*).
    • **nar** "they are" (*PE15:36*, *VT49:28-30*).
    • **nain**, listed as the plural of *na-* in *The Gnomish Lexicon* (*PE11:83*). In the mature Sindarin of *The Lord of the Rings* era, Tolkien abandoned the 1st and 2nd person forms in favour of the **zero copula**.

Past tense
  • **Derivation:** there are two possible stems.
    • **Basic past ne-:** from Common Eldarin *nē* (or reduplicated *nene* / *nane*), shortened in Old Sindarin to **ne-**. The stem already has *e*, so there is no further i-affection, and endings attach directly: **ne** "he/she/it was", **nen** "I was", **ner** "they were".
    • **Augmented past ane-:** Common Eldarin often formed past and perfect stems by prefixing the *sundóma* (the root vowel), a process Tolkien called **augmentation**: √KAR → *a-kar-*, √TUL → *u-túl-ie*, and so √NA → **a-ne-**. In Sindarin, the initial *a-* is kept, and the single medial *-n-* is a stable nasal that does not lenite, so endings attach directly to **ane-**.
  • **Attested forms:** Common Eldarin *anen*, *anel*, *anes* (*VT49:29*), which support the augmented stem.

Future tense
  • **Derivation:**
    • **Stem:** basic verbs form the future with the suffix *-tha* (*-atha* after consonant-final stems): √NA + *-tha* → **natha-**.
    • **Vowel change:** before pronominal endings, the final *-a-* of the stem becomes *-o-* (*nathon* "I will be"), while it is regularly kept as *-a* in the uninflected 3rd person singular (*natha*).
  • **Attested forms:** in his late draft notes for the *Túrin Wrapper* (*VT50:5*), Tolkien wrote the future copula as **natho** or **tho** "will be", and **nathon** "I will be" is attested directly (*VT50:5*). The attested 3rd person singular is therefore *natho* rather than the regular *natha*.

Imperative and non-finite forms
  • **Imperative no** "be!": from Primitive Eldarin *✶nā* → Old Sindarin *✶nō* → **no**. Besides commands, the imperative is also used to express wishes, possibilities, and hypothetical conditions ("may it be / let it be"):
    • **no aer i eneth lín** = "hallowed be thy name" (Sindarin Lord's Prayer, *Ae Adar Nín*, *VT44:21*)
    • Plural ("be ye!"): **nor** / **nogir**, if plural or pronominal suffixes are added to the imperative.
  • **Infinitive nei** (or **nae**) "to be": basic verbs add *✶-i*, with i-affection on the stem vowel (*✶na-i* → *nei*).
  • **Gerund nad** (or **naed**) "being": the common Sindarin word *nad* "thing" literally originated as this gerund of √NA ("that which is").
  • **Present participle naol** (or **neiel**) "being": from the suffix *-ol*, or historical *-iel* (*✶na-iel* → *neiel*).
  • **Past participle nanen** (or **nen**) "been": from the passive/perfect participle ending *-nen* attached to the root.

Whether this kind of paradigm should be used at all is up for debate. In its favour, Tolkien wrote few texts in Sindarin, and had he worked on the language further, he would probably have needed a "to be" paradigm sooner or later; the zero copula doesn't work all that well and is counter-intuitive. Against it, a language is only useful when people understand what's being communicated, and a paradigm that isn't widely recognised may remain unfamiliar to readers using other Neo-Sindarin conventions. Many Neo-Sindarin writers try to preserve as much of Tolkien's original ideas as possible, and a "to be" paradigm is too poorly documented for most of them to accept it. I think it depends on your audience. The Sindarin in the films seems to have worked, so although I don't use it much, I favour its adoption. This guide, however, will stick to the zero copula and ignore the "to be" paradigm from this point onwards, so we can focus on the flavour of Neo-Sindarin preferred by the majority of the community.
